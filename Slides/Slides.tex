\begin{frame}{Differences in Population \& Sample}
\phantomsection\label{differences-in-population-sample}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Population} is a large group of individuals, which a law of
  nature applies
\item
  \textbf{Sample} is a group of a given population intended to represent
  that population
\item
  \textbf{Participants} are those measured in a sample. Participants
  always refers to individuals (e.g., students, children, prisoners)
\item
  A sample is \emph{supposed to generalize} to a given population
\item
  We never use subjects in the social sciences anymore, they are
  participants
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Statistics vs Parameters}
\phantomsection\label{statistics-vs-parameters}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Statistics} use English letters to get values for a sample
\item
  \textbf{Parameter} use greek letters for values of a population
\end{itemize}

Statistic = Sample

Parameter = Population
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Is It a Normal Distribution?}
\phantomsection\label{why-is-it-a-normal-distribution}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Normal curve} is often called the bell-shaped curve; is
  symmetrical
\item
  \textbf{Normal Distribution} same thing as normal curve; represents
  the population because if you have enough data you will get a normal
  distribution (central limit theorem); if your data looks like this in
  a histogram, you're in good shape
\item
  \textbf{Distribution Tail} has two tails; these will be more important
  for statistics
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Skewed Distributions}
\phantomsection\label{skewed-distributions}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Negative Skew} is like a finger pointing left; not normal and
  is asymmetrical; indicates higher frequency of middle and higher
  scores; no low frequency in higher scores
\item
  \textbf{Positive Skew} is like a finger pointing right; not normal and
  is asymmetrical; indicates higher frequency of low and middle scores;
  no low frequency in lower scores
\item
  Some thresholds are that if you have a skewness value of +-2 or +-3
  then you're good to use that variable like it is.
\item
  \textbf{Kurtosis} is when your frequency are really skinny and tall or
  really flat and wide
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bimodal Distribution}
\phantomsection\label{bimodal-distribution}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bimodal Distribution} is when your distribution has two humps
  with a valley in the middle; high frequencies both below and above the
  middle of the plot
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Some Notes From JP}
\phantomsection\label{some-notes-from-jp}
Deciding what is positively and negatively skewed from visuals alone is
not good enough

You'll want to look into your descriptive statistics and look at
skewness and kurtosis to make sure they are within +-3

Some statistics can handle some skewness and kurtosis

Other times you'll have to transform the variable using fancy methods
that we will not talk about in this class.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Central Tendency}
\phantomsection\label{central-tendency}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Concept that as statisticians every person is just a data point
\item
  We are interested in the central score

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    We are interested in how much a person is away from that central
    score
  \item
    But when it comes to statistics we like to group together our
    participants' scores/values
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Measures of Central Tendency} are statistics that summarize
  the location of a distribution on a variable by indicating where the
  center is
\item
  A Normal distribution will have the central point right down the
  center
\item
  A skewed distribution will have the central point where the frequency
  of scores is the highest
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Median}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{The Mean/Average}
\phantomsection\label{the-meanaverage}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Mean} is the score located at the \texttt{mathematical} center
  of a distribution
\item
  Xbar is often used for the mean

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \[ \overline{X} \]
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \[\overline{X} = \frac{\Sigma\;X}{N} \] is the formula to calculate
  the mean.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Xbar is the Sum of X/Scores divided by the total number of
    observations/scores/values
  \end{itemize}

  Calculate the mean for interval and ratio scales

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The mean of ordinal/ordered data makes no sense
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Basis for most \texttt{inferential\ statistics}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Deviation}
\phantomsection\label{deviation}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  The distance a participant's score/value is from the mean/average
\item
  Deviations can be positive or negative

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    participants can score lower (negative) than the mean and higher
    (positive) than the mean
  \end{itemize}
\item
  To get the deviation, you subtract the mean from each participant's
  score
\item
  \[ X - \overline{X} \]
\item
  The larger the value the farther away from the mean the score/value is
\item
  \textbf{Sum of the deviations around the mean} is the sum of all
  differences between the scores and the mean
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking to the Future}
\phantomsection\label{looking-to-the-future}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Deviations is the start for upcoming lectures and statistical tests,
  especially the sum of the deviations
\item
  \[ \Sigma(X - \overline{X}) \]
\item
  If the sum of the deviations is 0 then that means your math is good
\item
  Deviation of each score/value from the mean is often referred to as
  error/residual in statistical tests
\item
  Correlational designs use the mean of IV and the mean of DV to look
  for a relationship between the two variables
\item
  Experiments compare the two or more groups (IV) and the relationship
  with the mean value/score of the DV
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Visuals}
\phantomsection\label{visuals}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  Line Graphs are good for showing progress or change in time
\item
  To show group differences (nominal or ordinal IV), bar graphs are the
  norm
\item
  Scatterplots are best for continuous IV and continuous DV (interval or
  ratio)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\end{frame}
